Home At a Glance About Me Projects

I didn't set out to study human behavior. I set out to build things. Somewhere between a cafeteria menu website I made in high school and a home-screen redesign I helped ship at Amazon, I started noticing the same question over and over — who is this actually for?

01 — Bugil High School, Korea

In my senior year of high school, I noticed the English-speaking teachers at my school packing lunch every day — they couldn't read the Korean cafeteria menu. I taught myself just enough code and the Google Image API to build a site that matched each Korean menu item to a photo of it. My psychology teacher told me she checked it every morning. That was the first time I understood: access to information is a quiet form of agency.

02 — Macalester College

Every spring, Macalester hosts a 24-hour hackathon called Macathon. My team built Beacon — an app that quietly detects when you dial 911 and sends your location to the call center, since old landline-era infrastructure often can't locate cell calls. We could've built something flashier, but in an emergency, the only thing that matters is one tap. We stripped it down to a single button. It won first place, and taught me that the best solution is often the simplest one a person can actually use under pressure.

03 — Concord USA

My first job out of college: building REST APIs and data integration pipelines between systems like SAP and Salesforce as a MuleSoft developer. When I noticed different client projects solving the same integration pattern from scratch, I built a reusable template so new developers could ramp up in days instead of weeks — my first lesson in fixing the root cause instead of patching around it.

04 — Amazon · Silk Browser

Six years on Amazon's Silk browser team. I shipped browsing history and autocomplete to millions of users, ran Silk's first Weblab-powered A/B experiment, delivered Safe Browsing on a brand-new OS, and led an infrastructure migration to AWS. I got promoted. I mentored newer engineers. By most measures, it was going well.

05 — The Turning Point

"We were optimizing for a proxy of value instead of the value itself."

We redesigned Silk's home screen, justified by one number: search volume. Meanwhile, the friction people actually complained about — clumsy cursor behavior, limited text editing — sat untouched. I understood the business case. I just couldn't unsee the gap.

06 — What's Next

I spent a stretch testing whether this pull toward human-centered design was real — finishing a Google UX Design Certificate on the side. It was, more than I expected. This September, I'm starting a Master's in Human-Centered Design & Engineering at the University of Washington, to make that question — who is this actually for — my actual job.
